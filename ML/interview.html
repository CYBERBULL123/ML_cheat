Answers :- Basic-Level Questions

1. What are Large Language Models (LLMs), and how do they work?

Answer:
Large Language Models (LLMs) are advanced artificial intelligence models trained on massive text datasets to understand and generate human-like language. They use deep learning techniques, particularly transformers, to process and predict sequences of words.

How They Work:

  1. Pretraining Phase – The model is trained on large corpora of text using self-supervised learning, predicting missing words or next words in a sequence (e.g., Masked Language Modeling in BERT, Autoregressive modeling in GPT).
  2. Fine-Tuning Phase – The pretrained model is further refined on domain-specific or task-specific datasets to improve performance on particular applications (e.g., customer service chatbots, code generation).
  3. Inference & Contextual Understanding – The model generates text based on input prompts by using statistical probabilities to determine the most likely next words. It can remember context within a prompt window, making it effective for applications like text summarization, translation, and question answering.

Key Technologies Behind LLMs:

  • Transformers: The architecture that enables parallel processing and long-range dependencies.
  • Self-Attention Mechanism: Helps models understand relationships between words across an entire text sequence.
  • Tokenization: Converts words or subwords into numerical representations.
  • Fine-Tuning & RLHF (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback): Helps improve accuracy and reduce biases.

2. Explain the basic architecture of GPT-based models.

Answer:
GPT (Generative Pre-trained Transformer) models are a class of transformer-based neural networks designed for natural language generation (NLG) tasks. They follow an autoregressive approach, meaning they predict the next word in a sequence given previous words.

Architecture Breakdown:

  1. Tokenization

    • Input text is tokenized into subword units using methods like Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE) or WordPiece.

  2. Embedding Layer

    • Tokens are converted into dense vector representations (embeddings) that capture semantic meanings.

  3. Stacked Transformer Blocks

    • The core of GPT consists of multiple transformer layers, each with:
      • Multi-Head Self-Attention (MHSA): Helps the model focus on different parts of the input sequence.
      • Feed-Forward Neural Networks (FFN): Processes attention outputs to learn complex patterns.
      • Layer Normalization & Residual Connections: Improves training stability and information flow.

  4. Positional Encoding

    • Since transformers lack a built-in sense of sequence order, positional encodings are added to embeddings to provide order awareness.

  5. Decoder-Only Design

    • Unlike BERT, which is a bidirectional encoder, GPT models use only the decoder of a transformer, making them autoregressive and unidirectional.

  6. Output Layer

    • A softmax function predicts the probability of the next token at each step.

GPT models (GPT-2, GPT-3, GPT-4) have increasingly larger architectures with billions of parameters, making them highly effective at text generation, completion, and reasoning tasks.

3. What is the difference between pretraining and fine-tuning?

Answer:

Pretraining and fine-tuning are two key stages in training large language models.

Feature Pretraining Fine-tuning
Objective Learn general language patterns from vast text corpora. Specialize the model on domain-specific or task-specific data.
Data Used Unsupervised, large-scale datasets (books, websites, Wikipedia). Labeled or curated datasets for specific applications (e.g., medical reports, chatbot dialogues).
Learning Type Self-supervised learning (e.g., predicting masked words or next words). Supervised or reinforcement learning (e.g., classification, summarization).
Example Training GPT on billions of internet text samples. Fine-tuning GPT for customer support or medical chatbot applications.
Computational Cost Very high (requires massive GPUs and TPUs). Comparatively lower, since only small task-specific adjustments are needed.

Fine-tuning allows models to adapt to specific use cases, improving accuracy and relevance for targeted tasks.

4. How do transformers improve upon RNNs and LSTMs?

Answer:

Transformers solve many of the limitations of Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks, making them the dominant architecture for NLP tasks.

Feature RNNs & LSTMs Transformers
Parallelization Sequential processing (slow). Fully parallelized, allowing much faster training and inference.
Long-Range Dependencies Struggles with long sequences due to vanishing gradients. Self-attention mechanism captures long-range dependencies effectively.
Memory Efficiency Needs hidden states for every step, increasing memory usage. Uses self-attention to process the entire input sequence at once.
Training Speed Slow due to sequential token processing. Faster due to parallel computation.
Attention Mechanism Limited to short contexts in LSTMs. Uses self-attention to weigh all words in the input sequence effectively.

The self-attention mechanism in transformers allows the model to consider all words in a sentence simultaneously, unlike RNNs/LSTMs, which process text word by word in a sequential manner. This innovation makes transformers vastly superior for modern NLP applications.

5. What is tokenization in NLP?

Answer:

Tokenization is the process of splitting text into smaller units (tokens), which are used as inputs for NLP models. These tokens can be words, subwords, or characters.

Types of Tokenization:

  1. Word Tokenization – Splits text into words (e.g., "Hello world!" → ["Hello", "world", "!"]).
  2. Subword Tokenization – Breaks words into smaller meaningful units using methods like Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE) or WordPiece (e.g., "unhappiness" → ["un", "happiness"]).
  3. Character Tokenization – Breaks text into individual characters (e.g., "ChatGPT" → ["C", "h", "a", "t", "G", "P", "T"]).
  4. Sentence Tokenization – Splits text into full sentences (e.g., "Hello world! How are you?" → ["Hello world!", "How are you?"]).

Most modern LLMs like GPT and BERT use subword tokenization (BPE/WordPiece) because it balances efficiency and flexibility, handling rare words and compound words effectively.

8. What are stop words, and why are they removed in NLP processing?

Answer:
Stop words are common words in a language (e.g., “is,” “the,” “and,” “in”) that carry little semantic meaning and are often removed to improve NLP efficiency.

Why Remove Stop Words?

  • Reduces computational cost – Less data to process.
  • Improves model accuracy – Focuses on meaningful words.
  • Enhances text analysis – Eliminates noise.

However, modern NLP models like transformers do not always remove stop words, as context matters in deep learning.

12. How does a Generative Adversarial Network (GAN) work?

Answer:
GANs are a type of deep learning model used for generating synthetic data. They consist of two neural networks competing against each other:

  1. Generator: Creates fake data (e.g., fake images, text).
  2. Discriminator: Evaluates if the data is real or fake.

How GANs Work:

  1. The Generator produces fake samples.
  2. The Discriminator tries to distinguish real from fake data.
  3. The Generator improves over time by trying to fool the Discriminator.
  4. After training, the Generator can produce highly realistic images, text, or music.

GANs are widely used in image synthesis, deepfake generation, and AI-based artwork creation.

15. How does diffusion-based Generative AI work?

Answer:
Diffusion models are a type of generative AI model that gradually refine an image (or data sample) by removing noise step by step. Unlike GANs, which use adversarial training, diffusion models learn to reverse a noise process to generate high-quality images.

How Diffusion Models Work:

  1. Forward Process (Noise Addition) – An image is gradually converted into pure noise.
  2. Reverse Process (Denoising) – The model learns to predict and remove noise to reconstruct the original image.
  3. Sampling New Data – Once trained, the model can generate new images by denoising random noise.

Key Advantages of Diffusion Models:

✅ Generate high-resolution images with great detail.
✅ More stable than GANs (no mode collapse issues).
✅ Used in DALL·E 2, Stable Diffusion, and Imagen.

Diffusion models have revolutionized AI-generated art, animation, and 3D object generation.

17. Explain the architecture of a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN).

Answer:

CNN Architecture Components:

  1. Convolutional Layers – Apply filters (kernels) to detect patterns like edges, textures.
  2. Activation Function (ReLU) – Introduces non-linearity, making the model more powerful.

CNNs power image classification (ResNet, VGG), object detection (YOLO), and facial recognition.

18. What is an activation function, and why is it important?

Answer:
An activation function introduces non-linearity into a neural network, allowing it to learn complex patterns.

Common Activation Functions:

Activation Formula Use Case
ReLU (Rectified Linear Unit) max(0, x) Most popular for deep networks.
Sigmoid 1 / (1 + e^(-x)) Used for binary classification.

24. What is the role of memory in AI agents?

Memory in AI agents refers to the ability of the agent to store and retrieve information from previous interactions or experiences. It plays a crucial role in enabling AI systems to exhibit more human-like behaviors, such as learning from past events, improving decision-making, and adapting to new situations over time.

In machine learning, memory is used to retain learned knowledge or context, which helps the agent make informed decisions based on historical data. This is particularly important in tasks like natural language processing (NLP), where understanding the context of a conversation over multiple turns can improve response quality. Memory can also enhance reinforcement learning, where agents learn from past experiences to optimize future actions. AI models like transformers may also have a “long-term” memory or state that influences future predictions.

There are various types of memory in AI:

  • Short-term memory: This holds information for a brief time, typically during a single task or interaction.
  • Long-term memory: More persistent storage that helps the agent remember knowledge over a longer period, facilitating generalization.
  • Working memory: This is where temporary and relevant data is held during an agent’s decision-making process.

Overall, memory is crucial for creating more sophisticated, adaptive, and context-aware AI agents.

25. What is multi-agent coordination in AI?

Multi-agent coordination in AI refers to the process where multiple autonomous agents work together to achieve a common goal or a set of goals. In multi-agent systems, the agents may have different roles, perceptions, and capabilities, but they need to align their actions and share information for collective problem-solving. Some key elements of multi-agent coordination include:

  • Communication: Agents need to exchange information to align their goals or strategies.
  • Task Allocation: Deciding which agent should take responsibility for a particular sub-task.
  • Synchronization: Ensuring agents’ actions are well-timed and do not interfere with each other.
  • Conflict Resolution: Managing disagreements when agents’ actions conflict or compete.
  • Cooperation and Competition: Balancing collaborative behavior with competitive strategies depending on the scenario.

Applications of multi-agent coordination are found in fields like robotics, where multiple robots work together on tasks like warehouse management or search and rescue missions. In gaming, AI agents may also coordinate in multiplayer settings.

26. What is prompt engineering, and why is it important?

Prompt engineering is the process of designing and refining prompts (inputs) that are given to language models (LMs) or AI systems to elicit the desired output. The goal is to craft prompts in a way that guides the model to produce accurate, relevant, and coherent responses. Effective prompt engineering can significantly improve the performance of AI models, especially in cases where fine-tuning the model is not an option.

Why it’s important:

  • Control: Well-crafted prompts provide better control over the model’s responses.
  • Efficiency: By adjusting the input, you can reduce the need for post-processing or multiple iterations to get the desired output.
  • Accuracy: A clear, well-structured prompt helps avoid ambiguity, leading to more accurate and useful results.
  • Task Specificity: In complex tasks, prompt engineering allows you to specify requirements and constraints clearly, helping the AI focus on the most relevant aspects.

For example, instead of asking a vague question like “What is the weather?”, you can use a more detailed prompt like “Provide the current weather in New York City with temperature, humidity, and precipitation.”

27. What is zero-shot, one-shot, and few-shot learning?

These terms refer to how much prior data or examples a model needs to effectively perform a task.

  • Zero-shot learning: In zero-shot learning, the model is asked to perform a task without having seen any labeled examples related to the task during training. The model relies on its general knowledge and understanding of similar tasks. For instance, a zero-shot model could answer a question about a topic it has never seen before by leveraging its general language capabilities and learned knowledge.

  • One-shot learning: In one-shot learning, the model is given just one example to learn from. This is common in tasks like image recognition where the model might only have one instance of an object to recognize in different contexts. One-shot learning is typically challenging but achievable with advanced techniques like Siamese Networks.

  • Few-shot learning: Few-shot learning is when the model is provided with a small number of examples (e.g., 5 or 10) to perform a task. This is the middle ground between zero-shot and one-shot learning. Few-shot learning is often used to improve the model’s generalization capabilities and is a common approach for fine-tuning large pre-trained models on specific tasks.

These learning paradigms are particularly useful for tasks where large amounts of labeled data are scarce or expensive to obtain.

28. What are common failures in prompt engineering?

Common failures in prompt engineering occur when the prompt is unclear, poorly structured, or does not align with the desired outcome. Here are some typical failures:

  • Ambiguity: A vague or ambiguous prompt can lead to unpredictable or irrelevant outputs.
  • Overloading: Asking the model to do too many things in a single prompt without clear instructions can result in confusion and poor responses.
  • Bias in prompts: The language used in prompts might unintentionally steer the model toward biased or skewed answers.
  • Lack of specificity: A prompt that lacks detail or context may produce generic answers that are not useful for the user.
  • Inconsistent formatting: If the prompt is not formatted correctly, the model may misinterpret it, leading to errors.

To avoid these failures, prompt engineering should focus on clarity, specificity, and structure, ensuring the model receives clear guidance on the expected response.

29. How do you structure a prompt for an LLM?

When structuring a prompt for a large language model (LLM), you should consider several factors to ensure that the model interprets the input correctly and produces the desired output:

  1. Context: Provide background information that is necessary for understanding the task. For instance, if you’re asking for a recommendation, clarify what criteria the model should consider.
  2. Instructions: Be clear and direct about what you want the model to do. Use action-oriented words like “Explain,” “Summarize,” or “Generate.”
  3. Format: If you need the output in a specific format (e.g., a list, a paragraph, or a table), indicate this explicitly.
  4. Examples: For tasks like translation, question-answering, or summarization, providing examples helps the model understand how to structure its response.
  5. Limitations: If there are any constraints (e.g., a word limit, focus on certain aspects), be sure to mention them in the prompt.

For example:

  • “Generate a short summary of the following article, focusing on the key points related to climate change impacts on agriculture.”
  • “Translate the following text from French to English.”

By providing structure, clarity, and context, you can help guide the model toward producing more relevant and accurate responses.

30. How does Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting improve responses?

Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting involves guiding the model through a step-by-step process to reach the final answer. Rather than expecting the model to produce an immediate response, CoT prompting encourages the model to break down the problem and explain its reasoning along the way.

How it improves responses:

  • Improved accuracy: By making the model explicitly reason through the steps, it can reduce errors and improve the quality of the answer, especially for complex tasks.
  • Transparency: CoT provides a clearer view of the model’s thought process, making the response more interpretable and explainable.
  • Handling complex tasks: For tasks that involve logical reasoning, multi-step calculations, or complex analysis, CoT ensures that the model doesn’t skip important steps.

For instance, instead of directly asking the model, “What is 78 times 23?”, CoT prompting would guide the model to explain how it would calculate the answer, breaking it down step-by-step:

  1. “First, multiply 70 by 20.”
  2. “Then, multiply 70 by 3.”
  3. “Now, multiply 8 by 20.”
  4. “Finally, multiply 8 by 3.”
    By following these steps, the model reaches the final answer with more precision and clarity.

CoT prompting can significantly improve the model’s performance on tasks requiring logical or mathematical reasoning, especially when the model is unsure of the correct approach.

Large Language Models (LLMs)

1. How do LLMs handle long-term dependencies in text?

LLMs, particularly transformer-based models, handle long-term dependencies in text through attention mechanisms. The attention mechanism allows the model to focus on different parts of the input sequence when generating an output, rather than relying solely on nearby tokens. Transformers use self-attention to weigh the importance of every token in the sequence relative to others, allowing them to capture both short-term and long-term dependencies effectively.

The Positional Encoding in transformers helps retain the order of tokens, which is essential for understanding context over longer distances. While LSTMs (Long Short-Term Memory) and GRUs (Gated Recurrent Units) are also designed to handle long-term dependencies, transformers have largely outperformed them due to their parallelization capabilities and more efficient attention mechanisms.

2. What is in-context learning in LLMs?

In-context learning is a type of learning where a language model makes predictions based on the context provided during the inference process, without needing explicit retraining. This means the model uses the current input (or a few examples given in the prompt) to adjust its behavior and generate relevant responses. The model does not change its underlying parameters, but rather adapts its predictions based on the patterns it learns from the prompt or task context.

For example, when a model is given an example of a sentence to translate followed by another sentence to translate, it infers from the first sentence how to proceed with the second one. In-context learning is beneficial for tasks like few-shot learning where examples are provided directly in the input.

3. Explain parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) techniques.

Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) refers to methods that allow fine-tuning a pre-trained model on specific tasks without modifying all of its parameters. Instead, PEFT techniques update only a subset of model parameters, making fine-tuning faster and computationally cheaper. These techniques are particularly valuable when working with large models, which can be expensive to train.

Some common PEFT techniques include:

  • Adapter layers: Small additional layers are added to the model, which are fine-tuned while keeping the original model’s parameters fixed.
  • Low-rank adaptation (LoRA): Modifies only a low-rank subset of parameters during training, reducing the number of parameters that need to be adjusted.
  • Prompt tuning: Fine-tunes the input prompts to guide the model’s behavior without altering its core parameters.

These methods allow models to adapt to new tasks with minimal computational overhead and without requiring a full retraining process.

4. What are hallucinations in LLMs, and how can they be mitigated?

Hallucinations in LLMs refer to instances where the model generates information that is factually incorrect or entirely made up. This happens because LLMs rely on probabilistic predictions based on the input context and sometimes produce outputs that sound plausible but lack grounding in reality.

To mitigate hallucinations, several strategies can be employed:

  • Fine-tuning: Models can be fine-tuned on high-quality, domain-specific datasets to improve factual accuracy.
  • External Knowledge Integration: Integrating external knowledge sources, such as knowledge graphs or databases, allows the model to verify its outputs.
  • Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF): Fine-tuning models with human feedback can help correct generated content by rewarding correct, fact-based answers and penalizing hallucinations.
  • Cross-checking: Combining outputs from multiple models or systems and cross-checking results can help reduce hallucinations.

5. How does instruction tuning work in LLMs?

Instruction tuning involves fine-tuning a pre-trained LLM on a dataset where the model is explicitly trained to follow instructions. This process typically involves creating a large set of examples where the input consists of a natural language instruction and the model’s response, guiding the model to learn how to produce outputs aligned with user intentions.

For instance, by training a model with instruction-based examples like “Translate the following sentence into French,” the model learns how to interpret and respond to commands more effectively. Instruction tuning allows LLMs to improve their ability to understand and generate responses in a way that aligns with user expectations, even when the instruction is vague or open-ended.

Natural Language Processing (NLP)

6. How does transfer learning work in NLP models?

Transfer learning in NLP involves taking a pre-trained model (typically on a large, general dataset) and fine-tuning it on a smaller, domain-specific dataset for a specific task. The key idea is that the model has learned general linguistic features (syntax, semantics, etc.) from a large corpus, which can be transferred to new tasks with relatively few task-specific examples.

For example, models like BERT or GPT are pre-trained on vast amounts of text data and can then be fine-tuned for specific NLP tasks like sentiment analysis, named entity recognition (NER), or machine translation. Transfer learning allows models to perform well on new tasks even with limited task-specific data.

7. Compare Word2Vec, GloVe, and FastText embeddings.

  • Word2Vec: Word2Vec generates word embeddings based on the context of words in a sentence. It uses either the skip-gram approach (predicting context words from a target word) or CBOW (Continuous Bag of Words) (predicting the target word from context). Word2Vec embeddings capture semantic relationships between words by learning from their surrounding context.

  • GloVe (Global Vectors for Word Representation): GloVe embeddings are based on matrix factorization techniques applied to the word co-occurrence matrix of a corpus. Unlike Word2Vec, which uses local context, GloVe takes into account the global context by utilizing co-occurrence statistics. This leads to embeddings that capture more global semantic properties.

  • FastText: FastText improves upon Word2Vec by representing words as bags of character n-grams. This allows FastText to handle rare words or out-of-vocabulary words by breaking them into smaller subword units, making it more robust to misspellings and morphologically rich languages.

8. What are the challenges in multilingual NLP?

Multilingual NLP faces several challenges:

  • Data scarcity: Many languages lack large, labeled datasets for training, making it difficult to build effective models for those languages.
  • Language diversity: Different languages have vastly different structures, syntax, and morphology, making it challenging to develop models that generalize across languages.
  • Word embeddings: Shared embeddings across languages may not work well for languages with distinct vocabularies, leading to poor cross-lingual transfer.
  • Bias: NLP models often exhibit bias toward the languages they are most trained on, leading to underperformance in less represented languages.

9. Explain attention mechanisms in NLP models.

Attention mechanisms allow NLP models to focus on different parts of the input sequence when making predictions. Instead of processing all input tokens equally, attention mechanisms compute a weighted score for each token based on its relevance to the task at hand. This dynamic weighting enables the model to prioritize important tokens and capture long-range dependencies in the text.

Self-attention is a key component of transformer architectures like BERT and GPT, where each token attends to all other tokens in the sequence. The self-attention mechanism computes attention scores by considering the relationships between tokens, allowing the model to understand context better and capture complex dependencies across long sentences or paragraphs.

10. What are transformer-based architectures, and how do they improve NLP models?

Transformer-based architectures (like BERT, GPT, and T5) rely on self-attention mechanisms and positional encodings to process sequences of text in parallel. Unlike RNNs or LSTMs, which process text sequentially, transformers can attend to all tokens in the input simultaneously, allowing them to capture long-range dependencies more effectively.

Key benefits of transformers:

  • Parallelization: Transformers process all tokens in parallel, significantly improving training efficiency compared to sequential models.
  • Scalability: Transformers are highly scalable, capable of handling large datasets and models (e.g., GPT-3).
  • Long-range dependencies: With attention, transformers capture long-range dependencies better than RNNs and LSTMs.

This architecture has revolutionized NLP, enabling state-of-the-art performance on various tasks like machine translation, question-answering, and text generation.

Generative AI (GenAI)

11. What are diffusion models, and how do they generate images?

Diffusion models are generative models that iteratively refine noisy data to generate new data points, like images. They start with random noise and progressively denoise it to form a clear image by learning the reverse process of adding noise to an image. The model is trained by first corrupting an image with noise and then learning how to reverse this process.

These models work well for tasks like image generation because they can generate high-quality images with more stable training compared to GANs (Generative Adversarial Networks). The process involves learning the likelihood of transforming noise into a meaningful image by conditioning the noise on the desired image properties.

14. What is reinforcement learning with human feedback (RLHF)?

Reinforcement Learning with Human Feedback (RLHF) is a learning paradigm where a model is trained using feedback from human evaluators. This feedback is used to improve the model’s decision-making process.

15. How do latent variables work in VAEs?

In Variational Autoencoders (VAEs), latent variables are variables that represent the underlying structure of the data in a lower-dimensional space. The VAE consists of an encoder and a decoder: the encoder maps input data to a distribution over the latent space (often a Gaussian), and the decoder reconstructs the data from these latent variables.

The purpose of the latent variables is to capture the most relevant features of the data in a compressed form. During training, VAEs learn to optimize both the reconstruction loss (how well the model reconstructs the input data) and the KL-divergence (how close the distribution of latent variables is to a standard normal distribution). This balance ensures that the model generates realistic samples when given a point in the latent space.

Latent variables help VAEs to model the underlying distribution of complex data, enabling them to generate new samples (e.g., images or text) that resemble the original data.

Deep Learning

16. What is batch normalization, and how does it help training?

Batch normalization is a technique used in deep learning to stabilize and speed up the training process. It works by normalizing the inputs to each layer in a neural network, ensuring that they have zero mean and unit variance. This helps mitigate issues related to vanishing or exploding gradients, which can slow down training and lead to poor model performance.

By normalizing the inputs at each layer, batch normalization reduces internal covariate shift, which occurs when the distribution of inputs to a given layer changes during training. This allows for faster convergence and reduces the need for careful initialization of parameters. Batch normalization has also been shown to have a slight regularization effect, which can help reduce overfitting.

17. Explain dropout and its importance in preventing overfitting.

Dropout is a regularization technique used in neural networks to prevent overfitting. During training, dropout randomly “drops” (sets to zero) a fraction of the neurons in a given layer at each iteration. This prevents the model from becoming overly reliant on any particular neuron and encourages the network to learn more robust features.

Dropout helps by making the network less sensitive to specific patterns in the training data, thus improving its generalization to unseen data. This stochastic behavior encourages the network to combine features in different ways during training, leading to a more diverse set of learned representations. Dropout is often used in fully connected layers of deep networks and can be applied with different probabilities (e.g., dropping 20% of neurons).

18. How does backpropagation work in neural networks?

Backpropagation is the process by which neural networks learn from errors during training. It involves computing the gradient of the loss function with respect to each weight in the network and updating the weights using an optimization algorithm like gradient descent.

The backpropagation process works as follows:

  1. Forward pass: The input data is passed through the network to compute the predicted output.
  2. Loss computation: The predicted output is compared to the actual target, and a loss (or error) is computed based on the difference.
  3. Backward pass: The error is propagated backward through the network, layer by layer, using the chain rule of calculus to compute the gradients of the loss with respect to each weight.
  4. Weight update: Using the gradients, the weights are adjusted in the opposite direction of the gradient (minimizing the loss function) by a small step (learning rate).

Through backpropagation, the model iteratively improves its parameters to minimize the loss function, which leads to better predictions.

19. What is self-supervised learning in deep learning?

Self-supervised learning is a type of machine learning where a model learns from unlabeled data by generating its own supervisory signal. In self-supervised learning, the model is typically tasked with predicting parts of the input data from other parts. This allows the model to learn useful representations from the structure of the data itself without relying on manually labeled datasets.

For example, in natural language processing, a model might be trained to predict missing words in a sentence (e.g., “The cat is on the ____”) based on the surrounding context. In computer vision, self-supervised learning can involve predicting the rotation angle of an image or completing missing portions of an image. Self-supervised learning is useful for leveraging large amounts of unlabeled data to pre-train models, which can then be fine-tuned on smaller labeled datasets.

20. What is knowledge distillation in deep learning?

Knowledge distillation is a technique where a smaller model (called the “student”) learns to mimic the behavior of a larger, more complex model (called the “teacher”). The goal is to transfer the knowledge from the teacher model to the student model so that the student can perform similarly well with fewer parameters, making it more efficient for deployment.

The student is trained by minimizing the difference between its predictions and the teacher’s predictions. This can include using soft targets (probabilities) from the teacher’s output rather than hard labels, which provides more nuanced information for the student to learn from. Knowledge distillation is useful for deploying large models in resource-constrained environments (e.g., on mobile devices or edge devices) without sacrificing much accuracy.

4. Explain gradient checkpointing in large-scale models.

Answer:
Gradient checkpointing is a memory-saving technique where intermediate results during forward passes are not stored, and are recomputed during the backward pass to save memory.

How Gradient Checkpointing Works:

  1. Forward Pass → The model performs the forward pass without storing intermediate activations (or checkpoints).
  2. Backward Pass → During backpropagation, the model recomputes the missing activations from the stored checkpoints.
  3. Memory Reduction → This reduces the memory footprint since fewer activations need to be saved.

Example Workflow:

In training a large deep learning model with many layers, instead of storing activations for all layers, only a few layers are saved as checkpoints. The rest are recomputed during backpropagation to optimize memory usage.

Real-World Uses:

  • Training Large Neural Networks on resource-constrained hardware like GPUs.
  • Deploying Deep Learning Models in environments where memory is a limiting factor.

Gradient checkpointing reduces memory usage and allows for training larger models without exceeding hardware limits.

8. What is contrastive learning in NLP?

How Contrastive Learning Works:

  1. Positive and Negative Pairs – The model is trained with pairs of inputs, where some pairs are similar (positive) and others are dissimilar (negative).
  2. Embedding Space – The goal is for similar pairs to have close embeddings in the feature space, while dissimilar pairs are further apart.
  3. Loss Function – A contrastive loss function (like triplet loss) ensures that the model minimizes the distance between similar pairs and maximizes the distance between dissimilar pairs.

Example Workflow:

In sentence similarity tasks, the model is trained to recognize that the sentences “I love dogs” and “I adore dogs” are similar (positive pair), while “I love dogs” and “Cats are cute” are dissimilar (negative pair).

Real-World Uses:

  • Recommendation Systems – Identifying similar items (e.g., movies or products) for personalized suggestions.

Contrastive learning helps models learn better representations by forcing them to understand the relationships between data points.

  • Architecture: GANs consist of two neural networks: a generator (creates fake data) and a discriminator (distinguishes fake from real data). They are trained adversarially.
  • Training Objective: The generator aims to create data that’s indistinguishable from real data, while the discriminator tries to identify whether the data is real or fake.

Comparison:

  • Use Case: Transformers excel at tasks requiring sequence generation (e.g., language generation), while GANs are used for image, audio, or video generation.
  • Training: Transformers are trained using maximum likelihood estimation, whereas GANs use a two-network adversarial setup.
  • Stability: GANs are known to suffer from training instability, while transformers, though complex, are often more stable in training.

Real-World Use:

  • Transformer Models – Text generation (e.g., GPT-3), machine translation.

14. Explain adversarial training in GANs.

Answer:
Adversarial training in GANs refers to the process where two neural networks, the generator and discriminator, are trained in opposition to each other, improving their performance through competition.

How Adversarial Training Works:

  1. Generator: Creates fake data (e.g., images).
  2. Discriminator: Tries to distinguish real data from fake data.
  3. Training Process: The generator improves by trying to fool the discriminator, while the discriminator improves by getting better at detecting fakes.
  4. Equilibrium: Ideally, the generator creates data so realistic that the discriminator can no longer tell the difference.

Example Workflow:

  • Step 1: The generator creates an image (fake).
  • Step 2: The discriminator evaluates the image and labels it real or fake.
  • Step 3: Based on feedback, the generator updates its weights to create more convincing images, and the discriminator updates its weights to improve detection.

Real-World Use:

  • DeepFake – Generating realistic video clips by training the generator to mimic real faces and the discriminator to identify fake faces.
  • Image Synthesis – GANs used to create art, fashion designs, and more.

Adversarial training helps refine models through competition, making GANs effective at generating realistic synthetic data.
